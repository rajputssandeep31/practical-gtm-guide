# Practical GTM guide and template

A workbook for choosing a market and bringing a B2B SaaS product to paying customers

Maintained by Sandeep Singh Rajput. Edition 1.1 prepared with AI assistance. Updated 8 October 2026.

## How to use it

Use this workbook to make the commercial and product decisions behind a launch, then turn those decisions into an activity plan. Work from a specific customer problem through the offer, buying process, first value and rollout. The outputs are an execution plan and a one page GTM brief supported by evidence, owners and explicit launch gates.

This edition is designed for a new B2B SaaS product or a material new offer. It includes self serve and sales assisted choices. For a consumer product, replace account level measures, procurement steps and buyer roles with the relevant household or individual journey.

Complete the customer and evidence sections first. Carry those decisions into pricing and distribution, then define what a customer must do to get value. Set release conditions before choosing an announcement date. Revisit earlier sections whenever a later decision contradicts them.

Write observed evidence separately from assumptions. When evidence is missing, name the next test and its owner. The examples illustrate reasoning; they are not market research or performance benchmarks. Replace every example and every bracketed field before using this for a real launch.

## Worked example

The worked example is a fictional vendor intake workspace. It collects required supplier documents, routes a request to finance and security, and shows who owns the next approval. Its first release supports one standard approval flow; it does not approve suppliers automatically or replace purchasing and payment systems. All customer descriptions, targets, prices and results in this workbook are illustrative assumptions. No completed research, experiment or customer outcome is claimed.

## 1 Launch decision and commercial goal

Decide what this launch must establish and how much the team can commit before that evidence exists.

Choose one offer, one initial segment and one review horizon. Separate the customer outcome from the commercial outcome. A release can ship successfully while failing to create repeat use or paid demand. A revenue objective also needs a time basis: new recurring revenue booked during the period is different from recurring revenue present at its end.

### Worked example

The fictional team will test a vendor intake workflow with operations teams that can begin without a custom integration. Its initial commercial hypothesis is five paying accounts within a 90 day review horizon. At an illustrative $600 monthly subscription, five accounts imply $3,000 monthly recurring revenue at the end of that period, assuming all remain active. This is a planning assumption, not a forecast.

## 2 Customer segment and buying trigger

Choose customers whose need, timing and ability to adopt fit this release.

An ideal customer profile is a selection rule, not a list of industries. Record the workflow, urgency, existing tools, approval constraints and accessible buyer. Compare a small number of candidate segments using the same evidence. Use company size only when it changes the problem or purchase path. State exclusions so sales and onboarding can reject poor fit consistently.

### Worked example

Candidate A has recurring supplier requests, an operations owner and an approval flow that can be configured without engineering. Candidate B needs a custom connection to its purchasing system before any pilot. The first release would prioritize A because its adoption path matches the product's current scope. The tradeoff is postponing larger contracts that depend on integration work; it is not a claim that A is the more valuable market.

## 3 Evidence and customer alternatives

Establish why a customer would change its current process and what evidence supports that belief.

Ask about the last time the job occurred: what started it, who did the work, what failed and what happened next. Capture a dated observation or source, the interpretation and a competing explanation. Interest in a demo is evidence of interest; a completed evaluation or a budget commitment answers a different question. Include the cost of continuing with the current approach and the cost of switching.

### Worked example

The fictional hypothesis is that requests stall because ownership and missing documents are hard to see. To test it, the team would examine anonymized workflow examples and ask operators to reconstruct a recent delayed request. If delays are mostly caused by supplier responsiveness, an internal approval dashboard may address only part of the problem. That finding would change both the feature promise and the evaluation plan.

## 4 Buyer roles and purchase path

Map who uses the product, who funds it and who can delay or block adoption.

For each role, document the outcome it cares about, its authority and the proof it needs. A champion may want faster work while finance needs a defensible budget and security needs deployment and data answers. Map the purchase path from problem recognition through evaluation, review, agreement and onboarding. A missing approver can matter more than another lead source.

### Worked example

In the fictional workflow, an operations manager could champion the evaluation, operators would use the workspace, finance could own the budget and security could review access and retention. The evaluation would need a defined sponsor and an agreed approval path before it starts. A partner who configures workflows would need a scoped handoff and explicit responsibilities.

## 5 Positioning and message proof

Explain why this customer should consider this offer against its current alternative.

Build the message from the chosen customer job. State the relevant alternative, the product's mechanism and the benefit you can support. Compare the product with the alternative the buyer actually uses. A feature becomes a claim only when you can explain how it changes the workflow. Maintain a claims register so public copy, demonstrations and sales materials make the same promise.

### Worked example

Proposed message for the fictional workspace: For operations teams managing vendor requests in email, this workspace collects required documents and shows the next approval owner in one shared queue. The supported demonstration is the routing and status workflow. Faster approval is a hypothesis to measure; the team would not advertise a percentage reduction without evidence.

## 6 Offer pricing and economics

Choose an offer a buyer can understand and the business can deliver sustainably.

Separate the unit of customer value from the billing model. Compare seats, workflow volume, accounts or a hybrid only when they plausibly track value and cost. Define inclusions, limits, overages, support, services and upgrade triggers. Check bill predictability and the effort needed to count usage. Test willingness to pay through offer choices or buying commitments; a cost calculation alone does not establish demand. [2]

### Worked example

The fictional offer would test a base subscription with an included request allowance. Per seat pricing might penalize inviting reviewers; pure usage pricing could make budgets harder to predict. An illustrative $600 monthly account with $180 of recurring direct delivery cost contributes $420 before acquisition and overhead. At $2,100 acquisition cost, simplified payback is five months if that contribution remains stable. These figures are assumptions, not recommended prices or benchmarks.

## 7 Sales motion and channel bet

Choose how the customer discovers, evaluates and buys the offer, with the required help available.

Distinguish a sales motion from an acquisition channel. Self serve, sales assisted and enterprise sales describe purchase and support paths; search, outbound and partners describe routes to an audience. Choose the motion around setup effort, approval requirements and price. Then choose a small channel test with a clear handoff. More channels create more work if the offer or evaluation path is still uncertain.

### Worked example

The fictional workspace would start with a sales assisted evaluation because approval routing needs configuration and a sponsor. A sample workflow can still be explored without a salesperson. The first channel test could be a focused webinar for operations teams using email intake. A broad paid campaign is an alternative, but would spend before the team knows which accounts can adopt and what proof converts them.

## 8 Evaluation and activation

Define the first customer result that demonstrates the product's value and how a buyer can reach it.

Separate sample exploration, evaluation, first live value and recurring use. A signup or feature click may be a necessary step without being activation. Choose an observable account level outcome and specify the window, prerequisites and exclusions. Design help around the hardest dependency: data, permission, another participant or integration. Evaluation terms should say what each party must contribute.

### Worked example

For the fictional workspace, a proposed activation event is one eligible vendor request submitted, routed to the required reviewer and given a recorded decision. Sample requests would measure exploration separately. The customer would supply its routing rule and invite a reviewer; the team would provide a setup session. A short trial is useful only if the customer's approval cycle allows the outcome to occur inside it.

## 9 Sales and partner enablement

Give the people selling or implementing the offer the material needed to make an accurate promise.

Build enablement around the evaluation and buying path. Sellers need fit rules, discovery prompts, a reliable demonstration and permitted claims. Partners need implementation scope, commercial responsibilities and an escalation route. Keep every asset tied to a buyer question or a handoff; a long slide deck does not establish readiness. Rehearse the actual sequence before accepting live opportunities.

### Worked example

The fictional demo would follow a missing document through submission, routing and reviewer decision. The seller would ask who owns intake and whether the standard route fits. If a buyer needs a custom purchasing system connection, the seller would use the exclusion rule instead of promising an integration date. An implementation partner would own configuration only where the agreement specifies that responsibility.

## 10 Launch readiness and support

Decide whether the product and operating team can deliver the promise to the next cohort.

Readiness follows from the offer's promise and expected customer workload. Define pass conditions and evidence for product behavior, billing, access, onboarding, support and incident handling. Distinguish a release blocking issue from a follow up improvement. Record the owner who can accept a residual risk and the limit of that acceptance. A marketing date does not resolve a missing operational capability.

### Worked example

Before the fictional cohort begins, the team would demonstrate the standard approval flow, verify that reviewers see only permitted requests, test invoice handling and rehearse a support escalation. Capacity would be limited by the team's ability to configure and support accounts. A billing defect or inappropriate access would block release; a cosmetic tutorial issue might be accepted with an owner and repair date.

## 11 Measurement and experiments

Learn where qualified accounts fail to get value or buy, and what intervention is worth testing.

Define events and cohorts before launch. B2B evaluation usually involves several people, so the identity unit and account mapping matter. Record denominators, exclusions and observation windows. Use mature cohorts when comparing conversion so recent entrants are not treated as failures. Funnel interpretation depends on event order and the conversion window; check how the chosen analytics tool implements them. [3]

### Worked example

A proposed funnel for the fictional offer is qualified account, evaluation accepted, configuration ready, first live value and paid account. If account readiness is the main delay, the team could test a configuration session against written instructions. Staff may choose the most promising accounts for help, so randomize eligible accounts when feasible; otherwise record assignment differences and treat the result cautiously.

## 12 Rollout gates and operating cadence

Sequence learning, investment and exposure so later work depends on earlier evidence.

Organize the rollout around decisions. Start with an offer and adoption test, move to a limited customer cohort, and increase reach after the product and buying path can support it. A stage needs a scope, owner, resource limit, exit evidence and a fallback. Set dates around dependencies and observed buying cycles. A calendar of campaigns is useful only after those constraints are understood.

### Worked example

The fictional team would first validate the job and package, then run a supported cohort using the standard approval flow. It would publish a broader announcement only after the demo, onboarding, billing and escalation path are ready. Expansion would depend on paid demand and repeat use, with a review of support effort. The same dates would not fit a segment with a longer procurement process.

## 13 GTM activities and execution plan

Translate the choices in Sections 1 to 12 into work that someone can deliver. Plan customer validation, product marketing assets, demand generation, sales and partner preparation, onboarding and commercial follow up. For every activity, specify the customer or internal audience, the output, one accountable owner, a due date, dependencies, effort or budget, and evidence of completion.

Work backward from the next release gate. Start with activities that resolve a critical uncertainty or unblock the buying path. A webinar needs an accurate offer, a demonstrated workflow, a tested registration route and capacity to handle qualified evaluations. Delay promotion when those dependencies are unresolved. Use separate owners for approval and execution when needed.

The following plan applies to the fictional vendor intake product. Day 1 is the start of its 90 day planning period; the ranges are illustrative work windows, not promised launch dates. Move a dependent activity when a gate slips. The initial acquisition test is one focused webinar with targeted invitations and follow up. Paid acquisition and a broad partner campaign are deferred until adoption and delivery economics justify them.

## Metric definitions

**Activation rate**

Eligible accounts reaching the defined first value event within the window / eligible accounts that entered the same cohort. Specify whether eligibility starts at evaluation acceptance or configuration readiness.

**Evaluation to paid conversion**

Paid accounts from a matured evaluation cohort / accounts in that cohort. State the conversion window and how pending decisions are handled.

**Time to first value**

Elapsed time between the defined start event and first value. Show the distribution and account coverage; do not hide incomplete journeys in a single average.

**Acquisition cost**

Included acquisition spending / new paying accounts attributable to that spending over an aligned cohort and period. State treatment of salaries, partner fees and attribution uncertainty.

**Gross margin**

Net revenue less included direct cost of delivery, divided by net revenue. State cost scope and period. This is different from operating profit.

**Simplified payback**

Acquisition cost per account / monthly contribution per account. The simple estimate assumes stable contribution and omits churn and timing effects; use a cohort cash model when those effects matter.
